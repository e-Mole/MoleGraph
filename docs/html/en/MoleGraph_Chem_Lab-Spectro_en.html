Transmittance (T)
Expresses the transmittance of a solution. It is the ratio of the light intensity that passed through the sample (I) to the light intensity that entered it from the light source (I₀):

T = I / I₀

It is often given in percentages (0–100%). However, as the concentration of a substance increases, transmittance does not decrease linearly, but exponentially.

Absorbance (A)
Describes how much light the solution absorbed. We obtain it by converting transmittance using the negative decadic logarithm:

A = -log₁₀(T)

Why do we plot the graph using Absorbance and not Transmittance?
Thanks to this conversion, the Beer-Lambert Law applies. It states that absorbance, unlike transmittance, is directly proportional to concentration. Thus, we get an easily readable line in the graph described by the equation:

A = ε · c · l

Where c is concentration, l is the path length (cuvette thickness), and ε is a specific constant, the molar attenuation coefficient, indicating how strongly a substance absorbs light of a given color. The higher it is, the more sensitive the measurement is even for low concentrations.
Practical application: If we use a standard cuvette with a thickness of exactly l = 1 cm, the equation simplifies to A = ε · c. The slope of our calibration line then corresponds directly to the coefficient ε. If we know its value from tables, we can calculate the unknown concentration without needing to create a calibration curve!
